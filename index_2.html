Part one · The problem

Your memory has a leak

Why the verses you learn in January are gone by Easter — and why it’s not your fault.

Everyone who has tried to memorize Scripture knows the pattern. You work hard on a verse, you have it word-perfect by Sunday — and a month later it’s a fog. It feels like a personal failing. It isn’t. It’s the normal, measurable behavior of every human memory.

In the 1880s a German scientist named Hermann Ebbinghaus spent years memorizing lists and testing himself at intervals. He discovered that forgetting follows a curve: steep at first, then flattening. You lose most of a new memory in the first days — not gradually over years.

The curve is the enemy — but it has a weakness. Every time you successfully recall something, the curve resets and flattens. Forgetting slows down. Everything below is built on exploiting that weakness, over and over, with perfect timing.

The takeawayForgetting isn’t a discipline problem. It’s a scheduling problem — and schedules can be fixed.
Part one · The science

The two discoveries that matter

A century of memory research boils down to two findings you can use tomorrow.

Discovery 1

Spacing beats cramming.

Ten minutes of review spread over a month builds far stronger memory than the same ten minutes in one sitting. This “spacing effect” is the most replicated result in learning science: a 2006 review pooled 839 comparisons across 317 experiments and 14,000+ people — spaced practice won across the board.

(Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2006)

Discovery 2

Recalling beats re-reading.

Struggling to produce a verse from memory strengthens it far more than reading it again. In a landmark study, students who practiced recall remembered dramatically more a week later than students who re-read — even though the re-readers felt more confident. The effort of retrieval is what does the work.

(Roediger & Karpicke, Psychological Science, 2006)

Put the two together — spaced, effortful recall — and memory stops leaking.

Part two · The tool

Meet Anki: it knows the curve

A free flashcard app that does all the scheduling for you.

Anki (free on computer and Android; a one-time purchase on iPhone) holds your verses as digital flashcards. Each day it deals you a small stack: any new verse you’re learning, plus the handful of old ones due for a check-in. You try to say each verse aloud, flip to check yourself, and answer with one tap — and that tap is the whole “scheduling” you ever do:

That stretching is why the workload stays tiny. A verse you’ve known for two years might check in once every few years — nearly free to keep. Your daily stack stays small even when your collection grows into the thousands, because almost all of it is “asleep” at long intervals.

Part two · The brains: FSRS

A weather forecast for your memory

The algorithm inside Anki, explained without a single equation.

How does Anki know a verse is about to slip? Since 2023 it has shipped with a scheduler called FSRS — the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler — built by an open community of researchers and trained on hundreds of millions of real people’s real reviews.

The easiest way to understand it: FSRS is a weather forecast — except instead of predicting rain, it predicts the chance you can still recite a verse. For every card, every day, it tracks how hard that verse is for you, how long it tends to stick, and the live probability you could say it right now. When that probability is about to dip to your target — 90% by default — the verse lands in tomorrow’s stack. Not before (wasting your time), not after (letting it slip). Right on the line.

How do we know it works?

FSRS is open source, and its makers run a public benchmark: the review histories of roughly ten thousand real users — hundreds of millions of reviews — scored on one question: how well did you predict whether this person would remember this card today?

Part three · The technique

The first-letter method

A centuries-old memorizer’s trick that fits perfectly inside a flashcard. Try it below.

Reciting a whole verse from nothing can feel like a cliff. The first-letter method turns the cliff into a staircase: write out only the first letter of every word, keeping the punctuation. That skeleton is too thin to read — but it’s just enough to catch you when you stumble.

Scripture · live demo

Why does something so thin work so well? It gives you the rhythm, the word count, the shape of the sentence — the things that jog a true memory. It does not give you the words, so your brain still does the retrieval — which is exactly the effort that builds the memory. A cue, not a crutch. In the deck, the hint stays hidden by default, appears on a tap, and is generated automatically from the verse text.

One rule keeps it honestAlways attempt the verse cold first. The hint is a ladder for the hard days — not the floor you stand on.
Part three · The deck

Anatomy of a Scripture card

Small design choices that turn loose verses into passages you can actually recite.

Each card cues you with only the reference — the purest form of recall. Above and below your verse, in quiet gray, sit the verse before and the verse after. You aren’t just learning islands — you’re learning the handoffs between verses, which is what lets you recite a whole chapter as one flowing passage. (The context resets at each book, so James never bleeds into 1 Peter.)

A smart learning order

Start with short, beloved books — James, Philippians, 1 Peter — for early wins. Then Psalms & Proverbs, the doctrinal letters, the Gospels. The deck serves them in sequence; you never decide what’s next.

Whole-chapter cards

Sprinkled through the deck are cards that ask you to recite an entire chapter, start to finish. Spaced repetition itself schedules your recitation practice — the goal isn’t flashcards, it’s fluency.

Part four · The payoff

What one verse a day becomes

Small, boring, daily — and it quietly compounds into something astonishing.

Year 1: 365 verses — the length of James, Philippians, 1 Peter & Titus. Year 10: about all four Gospels. Year 22: the entire New Testament. Year 31: add Psalms & Proverbs.

And the cost? Reviews average two to three minutes a day — and stay there. Even after sixteen thousand verses, the heaviest day of the year is about four minutes, because nearly all of your collection is resting at intervals of months or years.

One verse a day is the pace of someone who finishes. It survives busy seasons, travel, and toddlers. Miss a day and nothing piles up — tomorrow simply brings the next verse.
